# The Critical Swing Voter — 2026 Construction

> **Status: constructed persuasion target.** This is a composite profile for testing
> angles and messages against, not a real person and not survey output. It is built for
> the 2026 electoral coalition (see elections-narratives.md — the "no longer applies"
> flags) rather than the 2020 crossover coalition. Review and sharpen as polling and
> focus-group evidence arrives; where real research contradicts this document, the
> research wins.

## Who they are

A composite of the three voter movements that decide 2026:

1. **The 2023 defector coming home-or-not** — voted Labour in 2020 (many for the first
   time), switched to National in 2023 on cost of living and "time for a change", and
   is now disappointed: prices still rising, mortgage repriced upward, services
   visibly thinner. Not proud of either vote. Deciding between returning to Labour,
   staying with National reluctantly, or drifting to NZ First/minor parties as a
   protest.
2. **The demoralised 2020 Labour voter** — stayed home or party-voted Green in 2023.
   Persuadable back with a concrete offer, but allergic to being taken for granted.
   Turnout risk, not switching risk.
3. **The Māori-roll voter weighing Labour vs Te Pāti Māori** — in the seven Māori
   electorates, the contest is on Labour's left flank, not its right. Different
   register applies (see below).

The centre-of-gravity persona for message testing is #1. Assume:

- **Mid-30s to mid-50s, mortgaged or renting-and-priced-out, suburban or provincial**
  (Hutt Valley / outer Auckland / Hamilton / Christchurch belt). Household income near
  the median; feels poorer than three years ago and can name why to the dollar.
- **Time-poor and news-light**: consumes politics via headlines, social clips, talkback
  fragments, and the supermarket shelf. Sees maybe one political message a day. It has
  to work in eight seconds.
- **Values, in order**: household security (mortgage/rent, groceries, power), access to
  a GP and a hospital that works, their kids' prospects (jobs, training, a path to a
  home), safety in town, and being respected rather than lectured.

## How they reason (the persuasion physics)

- **Retrospective, not ideological.** They ask "am I better off, and whose fault is
  that?" — not "which philosophy do I share?" In 2023 they answered it against Labour;
  the 2026 question is whether National has earned the same verdict. Every effective
  message either builds that verdict or shows Labour's offer in groceries-and-GP terms.
- **Promise-sceptical by direct experience.** They have now watched BOTH parties
  promise cost-of-living relief and fail to deliver it. A Labour promise is not
  believed by default: it needs a number, a date, and a funding line, or it reads as
  the same old politics. Small-and-certain beats big-and-vague every time.
- **Loss-averse.** Fear of rents/rates/taxes rising under Labour is National's best
  weapon against them. Reassurance on what Labour will NOT do (settled, simple tax
  position) is as load-bearing as the offer itself.
- **Allergic to culture war from either direction.** Nuclear ships, flags, ideological
  fights read as "not focused on me". The tested counter — acknowledge briefly, pivot
  to household costs — matches how this voter actually feels ("families aren't lying
  awake at night wondering about X").
- **Anti-politics baseline.** Assumes politicians dodge, blame, and spin. Specificity,
  admission of limits, and naming real places/people cut through; jargon, process
  answers ("we have announced a review"), and gloating do not. They notice when a
  politician answers the actual question.
- **Fairness frame over poverty frame.** They respond to "working people playing by
  the rules are going backwards while speculators and tobacco companies get tax
  breaks" — an US-vs-rigged-system story that includes them. Pure hardship framing
  ("the most vulnerable") reads as about someone else and can even trigger "what
  about me".

## What moves them TOWARD Labour (tested against the 2026 message architecture)

- Concrete priced offers they can picture using within weeks of the election: free
  GP visits, the $20 fare cap, power-bill savings. The test: can they say what they
  get, in one sentence, unprompted?
- The broken-promise ledger against National — but only when anchored to their own
  receipts (food prices, mortgage, GP fee), not abstract statistics. "White bread up
  28%" works; "CPI at X%" does not.
- Local proof: a named clinic, a closed mill, their line's fare. National-scale claims
  land only when they are visibly true in their suburb.
- Competence signals: discipline, costed policy, no internal chaos. They punished
  Labour's 2023 disarray; they will notice its absence.

## What pushes them AWAY (traps for the angle generator)

- **Tax ambiguity.** Any daylight between Labour spokespeople on tax/fiscal questions
  confirms National's core attack. This is the single highest-risk message surface
  (see narratives doc: 2017 and 2023 both).
- Relitigating 2020–2023. Defending the last Labour government's record reads as
  refusing to listen to why they left. The stance is forward-facing: "you were right
  to be frustrated — here's what's different."
- Mockery or personal attack on Luxon/Willis. They may dislike the government, but
  ridicule reads as Wellington-bubble smugness and violates the campaign philosophy
  anyway. Attack outcomes and choices, never persons.
- Anything that smells like spending without a price tag, or "free stuff" without a
  funding line — triggers the loss-aversion reflex.
- Being told their concerns (crime, immigration pressure on services) are wrong or
  bigoted, rather than answered with a Labour plan.

## Māori-roll variant (for Māori-electorate items only)

Different contest, different physics: the swing is Labour ↔ Te Pāti Māori, judged on
delivery for whānau (housing, hauora, rangatahi jobs) vs voice/advocacy strength.
Cost-of-living material still leads, in whānau terms; Crown-relationship attacks on
National land well; attacks on TPM mostly do not — contrast on delivery and
electability instead ("a vote that changes the government"). Register: warm, concrete,
kanohi-ki-te-kanohi credibility over studio polish.

## How to use this profile

When generating or triaging angles: score persuasion against persona #1 by default.
An angle "lands" only if it survives three questions in their voice — *"what do I
get?"*, *"why should I believe you this time?"*, and *"who's paying for it?"*. If an
item is Māori-electorate-specific, use the variant. If an angle only works on people
who already vote Labour, say so — base-pleasing is not persuasion.
