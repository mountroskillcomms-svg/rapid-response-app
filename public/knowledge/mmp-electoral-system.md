# How New Zealand's Electoral System Works (MMP)

> Knowledge base for the Rapid Response tool. Verified against Elections NZ boundary-review
> figures, July 2026. This is stable structural knowledge — it changes only via legislation
> or the periodic boundary review.

## The basics

New Zealand elects its Parliament using **MMP — Mixed Member Proportional** — adopted by
referendum in 1993 and first used in 1996, and reconfirmed by referendum in 2011.

Every voter gets **two votes**:

1. **Party vote** — for a political party. This is the vote that (mostly) determines the
   overall share of seats each party gets in Parliament. It is by far the more consequential
   vote.
2. **Electorate vote** — for a candidate to represent the voter's local electorate,
   decided by simple plurality (first past the post within the electorate).

## Parliament's composition — 2026 election specifics

- **120 seats** in the House of Representatives (baseline; can grow via overhang, below).
- For the **2026 election (7 November 2026)**: **71 electorates** — **64 general
  electorates** and **7 Māori electorates** — plus **49 list seats**. (The 2025 boundary
  review moved one North Island general seat to the list: 72→71 electorates, 48→49 list
  seats, relative to 2023.)
- Māori electorates overlay the whole country geographically; voters of Māori descent
  choose (at enrolment-roll option times) whether to be on the general or Māori roll.

## How seats are allocated

1. Count the nationwide party vote.
2. **Thresholds**: a party's party vote only counts toward seat allocation if it either
   wins **at least 5% of the party vote** OR **wins at least one electorate seat** (the
   "electorate lifeboat" / one-seat rule).
3. Qualifying parties' votes are fed into the **Sainte-Laguë formula** (divisors 1, 3, 5,
   7, …) to distribute all 120 seats proportionally.
4. Each party's electorate winners take their seats first; the remainder of that party's
   entitlement is filled from its **party list**, in list order, skipping anyone who
   already won an electorate.
5. **Overhang**: if a party wins more electorate seats than its proportional entitlement,
   it keeps them all, and Parliament temporarily grows beyond 120 (e.g. 121–123 seats in
   some terms). Other parties' seat counts are unaffected.

## Practical implications (the ones that matter for comms)

- **The party vote decides governments.** "Two ticks" campaigning exists because winning
  electorates without party vote gains adds nothing to a party's total (unless overhang).
- **Electorate wins matter disproportionately for small parties** near the 5% line: one
  electorate seat brings in list MPs proportional to the party vote (e.g. the classic
  Epsom arrangement for ACT). This is why "coat-tailing" is a recurring campaign story.
- **A wasted vote argument** applies to parties polling under 5% with no winnable
  electorate: their party votes are discarded and effectively redistributed to
  everyone else.
- **Coalitions are the norm.** No party has governed alone under MMP except Labour's 2020
  outright majority (65 seats) — the only single-party majority in the MMP era.
  Post-election negotiations between parties normally determine the government.
- **List MPs and electorate MPs have equal status** in the House. Losing an electorate but
  ranking high on the list still returns an MP ("dual candidacy").
- **By-elections** occur only for electorate vacancies; list vacancies are filled by the
  next available person on the party's list.

## Government formation

- The Governor-General appoints as Prime Minister the person who commands the
  **confidence of the House** — established through coalition agreements and/or
  confidence-and-supply agreements after the election.
- Caretaker convention governs the period between election day and government formation.

## Key electoral administration facts

- Elections run by the **Electoral Commission** (elections.nz). Electoral law: Electoral
  Act 1993.
- Parliamentary term: 3 years (maximum).
- **Advance voting** typically opens roughly two weeks before election day and now
  accounts for around half of all votes cast — campaign timing must treat the final
  fortnight as election "days", not run-up.
- Election-day rules: no campaigning of any kind on election day itself (s197 offences);
  advertising blackout applies. All campaign advertising requires a promoter statement.
- **Boundary reviews** by the Representation Commission follow each census (most recent:
  2025 review, finalised 8 August 2025, in force for the 2026 election).
- Official results (including specials) follow preliminary results by about two weeks;
  special votes have historically shifted 1–2 seats, typically leftward.
