# The Last Three Elections — Narratives, Outcomes, and What Transfers to 2026

> **Status: interpretive background context.** Unlike the structured databases in this

> judgement to reason FROM, never quoted as fact in output. Review and amend before
> relying on it heavily. Results figures are official; narrative assessments are
> editorial.
>
> How to use: when generating strategy, angles, or framing advice, draw on the

## 2017 — "Let's do this": the change election that nearly wasn't

**Result:** National 44.4% (56 seats), Labour 36.9% (46), NZ First 7.2% (9), Greens
6.3% (8). NZ First chose Labour; Ardern became PM leading a Labour–NZF coalition with
Green confidence and supply.

**The narratives and how each side played them:**

- **Generational change vs steady hand.** Labour entered the campaign polling in the
  low 20s under Andrew Little; his resignation seven weeks out and Ardern's elevation
  ("Jacindamania") transformed the race within days. Labour ran relentless positivity
  — "Let's do this", "the future of work", climate as "my generation's nuclear-free
  moment" — an optimism-and-renewal frame against a tired third-term government.
  National ran on economic management and stability under Bill English: solid, no
  vision offer.
- **Housing crisis and child poverty.** Labour owned these as moral failures of a
  decade of National government. Persuasive as diagnosis; KiwiBuild as prescription
  later became a liability (see 2020/2023).
- **The "$11.7 billion fiscal hole".** Steven Joyce's attack on Labour's fiscal plan
  was rejected by nearly every independent economist, but it dominated a week of
  coverage and forced Labour onto defence about tax and spending. Labour's decision to
  defer its tax working group ("no new taxes from it before 2020") was a direct
  concession to this pressure.
- **Winston as kingmaker.** The campaign's final act was decided in coalition
  negotiation, not on election night. National "won" the election and lost the
  government.

**What played out / persuasiveness verdict:** positivity + fresh leadership beat a
competent but visionless incumbent — but only once the leader could carry it. The
fiscal-hole attack shows a false claim can still shape a campaign week if the rebuttal
is slower than the news cycle.

**Transferable learnings for Labour 2026:**

1. **Vision beats management when voters want change — and 2026 Labour is the change
   option.** The 2017 positions are reversed: National is now the incumbent running on
   management amid worsening conditions. Labour's 2017 playbook (hope, renewal,
   concrete moral failures of the government) is more relevant to 2026 than anything
   from 2020 or 2023.
2. **Rebut fiscal attacks within the same news cycle, with independent validators
   lined up in advance.** The lesson of the fiscal hole is speed and third-party
   credibility, not correctness. Expect a 2026 rerun against Labour's costings (the
   deferred Future Funds costings are the obvious target).
3. **Tax ambiguity is where Labour campaigns go to die.** 2017's tax-working-group
   wobble previewed 2023's wealth-tax confusion. The rule: whatever the tax position
   is, it must be settled, simple, and repeated without variation from day one.

**No longer applies:**

- **The "Jacinda effect" is not replicable and should not be waited for.** A
  seven-week leadership lightning strike rescued 2017; treating charisma as a strategy
  is survivorship bias. 2026 must be won on issue terrain, not personality wave.
- **Winston-as-kingmaker maths has changed shape**: NZ First is in government with
  National this time and has ruled out working with Labour under Hipkins. Coalition
  signalling in 2026 is Labour–Greens–TPM vs National–ACT–NZF — a bloc-vs-bloc
  election, not a courtship of the centre party.

## 2020 — "Team of five million": the competence landslide

**Result:** Labour 50.0% (65 seats) — the only single-party majority of the MMP era.
National 25.6% (33) after a year of leadership churn (Bridges → Muller → Collins).
ACT rose from 1 seat to 10; Greens 7.9%.

**The narratives and how each side played them:**

- **Crisis competence.** The election was a referendum on the COVID response: hard
  early lockdown, "go hard, go early", daily podium communication, "be kind", "team of
  five million". Labour asked for a mandate to "keep moving" and got a landslide.
- **National's collapse was self-inflicted** — leadership instability, leaks, a
  border-numbers stumble-a-week campaign. The opposition's unfitness was as big a
  story as the government's competence.
- **Small-target policy.** Labour ran on almost no new policy — the safest possible
  offer to bank a competence dividend. It worked electorally and cost strategically:
  the majority came with no mandate for major reform, and the second term struggled to
  convert unprecedented power into durable, popular achievements.

**What played out / persuasiveness verdict:** competence-in-crisis is the most
powerful electoral force in NZ politics when it is genuinely on display — and it is
entirely conditions-dependent.

**Transferable learnings for Labour 2026:**

1. **Delivery credibility is cumulative.** The 2020 high-water mark was spent by 2023
   partly because flagship programmes (KiwiBuild, light rail, mental-health rollout)
   under-delivered against their announcements. For 2026 this cuts in Labour's favour
   applied to National: relentlessly prosecute the gap between what National promised
   (cost of living down, 50,000 fewer on Jobseeker, mortgage rates) and what happened.
   That is the exact weapon that beat Labour in 2023.
2. **An opposition in visible disarray forfeits the election.** 2020 National shows
   what churn and error-rate do under campaign scrutiny. Discipline — one story per
   day, zero unforced errors — is itself a narrative.
3. **Beware the small-target trap in reverse.** Labour 2026 needs enough concrete,
   costed retail policy to be the change option (fare caps, GP visits, SolarSaver are
   this cycle's versions), because "we're not them" alone did not sustain governing
   authority even after a landslide.

**No longer applies:**

- **Everything conditional on the pandemic.** The rally-round-the-flag effect, the
  podium incumbency advantage, the permission for extraordinary spending — none of it
  transfers. Treat 2020 as an outlier in every quantitative sense (vote share,
  turnout dynamics, single-party majority).
- **"Be kind" as a register.** The 2026 corpus (see tone guide) is sharper and more
  combative; the electorate's patience for soft-focus messaging ended with the
  cost-of-living era.

## 2023 — "Back on track": the cost-of-living reckoning

**Result:** National 38.1% (48), Labour 26.9% (34) — a 23-point collapse from 2020.
ACT 8.6% (11), Greens 11.6% (15), NZ First 6.1% (8) returned, TPM 6 electorate seats.
National–ACT–NZF coalition formed.

**The narratives and how each side played them:**

- **Cost of living owned the election.** Inflation peaked over 7% in 2022 and stayed
  high through the campaign; mortgage rates repriced household budgets. National's
  "get New Zealand back on track" fastened every price rise to Labour's spending.
  Labour never found a persuasive counter: its record frame ("we got you through
  COVID") read as backward-looking, and its cost-of-living offers (GST off fruit and
  veg) were widely panned as gimmicks — including, leaked, by its own ministers.
- **Tax chaos.** Hipkins' "captain's call" ruling out a wealth tax/CGT mid-campaign,
  after documents showed one had been worked up, managed to alienate the left flank
  AND fail to reassure the centre — worst of both worlds. National's tax-cut offer,
  despite contested costings (foreign-buyer revenue), stayed simple and survived.
- **Crime and ram raids.** A sustained two-year drumbeat (retail crime, youth
  offending) that National and ACT rode hard. Labour's responses read as reactive
  concessions to the frame, never a counter-frame.
- **Co-governance backlash.** Contested but real: Three Waters and co-governance
  became a proxy fight the government kept losing on definition — the policy was
  never successfully explained in public, so opponents defined it.
- **The policy bonfire.** Hipkins' early-2023 reset (scrapping unpopular policies)
  bought a poll bounce, then faded: jettisoning your own agenda concedes the
  opposition's critique without gaining an affirmative story.
- **Incumbency fatigue + Ardern's January resignation** removed the personality
  asset the party had built two elections around, exposing how little issue-ground
  had been banked underneath it.

**What played out / persuasiveness verdict:** the simplest economic frame won.
"You're worse off, they did it, we'll fix it" beat every attempt at nuance. Attacks
on National's costings didn't land because the underlying mood (change, relief) was
settled early.

**Transferable learnings for Labour 2026 (the most directly applicable set):**

1. **Run National's 2023 playbook back at them.** "They promised to fix the cost of
   living; you're worse off" is structurally identical to what beat Labour — and the
   current press-release corpus shows Labour already running it. The learning from
   2023 is that this frame wins when the lived experience confirms it *and the
   messenger repeats it with total discipline for the full cycle*. Never let a week
   pass without fastening a price to a National choice.
2. **Simplicity beats correctness in economic argument.** National's contested
   tax costings survived expert criticism because the offer was simple. Labour's 2026
   retail offers (three free GP visits, $20 fare cap, SolarSaver) have the right
   shape; resist any temptation to complicate or caveat them. Conversely, the
   deferred Future Funds costings are a standing vulnerability — have the defensive
   line settled before it is prosecuted.
3. **Settle internal tax/fiscal questions before the campaign, in private, once.**
   Mid-campaign captain's calls read as chaos. Whatever the position, every
   spokesperson must give the same one-sentence answer for six months.
4. **Never fight on the opponent's definition.** Co-governance and crime were lost at
   the framing stage, not the policy stage. In 2026, watch for National picking
   culture-war terrain (e.g. the nuclear-free probe already visible in the corpus) to
   pull Labour off cost-of-living ground — the corpus response ("families aren't
   lying awake at night wondering about X") is the correct counter: acknowledge,
   reframe to living costs, refuse the bait.
5. **A late reset can stabilise but cannot win.** The policy bonfire teaches that
   corrections must come early enough to build a new story, not just stop a bad one.
   Applied to 2026: any repositioning needed must be done NOW (pre-writ), not in
   September–October.

**No longer applies:**

- **Labour as tired incumbent.** Every 2023 dynamic that punished Labour — record
  fatigue, "time for a change", owning every price — now points at National. Labour
  is the challenger: lighter on record-defence, freer to attack, judged on offer
  rather than delivery. Strategy documents that still reason from a defensive crouch
  are fighting the last war.
- **The Ardern-era coalition of the centre.** The 2020 crossover voters (soft
  National, provincial, older) mostly went home in 2023. The 2026 path runs through
  turnout and persuasion on cost-of-living/health/housing, plus the Māori seats
  contest with TPM — a different electoral coalition, and the swing-voter profile
  (profiles/ folder) should be built for it, not for 2020's.
- **COVID-era permission for big spending.** Fiscal credibility attacks now land
  harder; every offer needs a visible price tag and funding line (the corpus already
  does this — "costed at an average of $56.5m a year, funded through future operating
  allowances").

## The one-paragraph synthesis

Across three elections the pattern is consistent: **the side with the simplest frame
matched to the electorate's dominant lived experience wins** — hope-and-change against
a visionless incumbent (2017), competence in crisis (2020), economic relief against a
blamed government (2023). Campaigns lose when they fight on the opponent's definition,
leave tax questions ambiguous, or defend records instead of prosecuting a story about
the future. 2026 hands Labour the structurally favourable side of the 2023 frame —
challenger prosecuting cost-of-living failure — and the discipline lessons of its own
defeats are the manual for using it.
